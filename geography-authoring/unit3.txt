Land cover, land use and anthropogenic biomes|Land cover describes the physical surface, such as forest, crops, water or buildings; land use describes its purpose. Anthropogenic biomes are ecological patterns strongly shaped by people and their activities.|A forest can be used for conservation or timber production; both may initially appear as forest cover. Cropland and urban settlement alter ecosystem structure and flows, but human influence also exists in apparently natural landscapes.|Classroom paired maps: one labels forest and grass; the other labels reserve, grazing and residential land. A forest parcel changes from reserve to plantation without immediately changing its cover class.|Distinguish physical cover from management purpose before measuring change. A cover map alone cannot establish legal land use or ecological quality. Investigate species composition and management to assess the transformation.|Land cover and land use are interchangeable labels.|cover|land cover;land use;anthropogenic biome;ecosystem
Deforestation and agricultural expansion|Deforestation converts forest to another cover; degradation reduces forest condition without necessarily removing its mapped forest class. Agricultural expansion increases area; intensification increases inputs or output on existing land.|New fields replace habitat and change carbon, water and soil processes. Intensification may reduce expansion pressure in some contexts but can increase fertiliser use, water demand or profitable expansion elsewhere.|Classroom region: forest declines from 800 to 620 km² while cropland expands by 150 km² and settlements by 30 km² over ten years.|Forest loss is 180 km², or 22.5% of initial forest; average net loss is 18 km² per year. The accounting balances, but it does not show degradation within remaining forest or the timing of clearing.|Higher farm yields always stop forest clearing.|cover-change|deforestation;degradation;expansion;intensification
Urban growth and mining transformation|Urban growth replaces or modifies vegetation and soils with buildings, roads and other infrastructure. Mining may remove vegetation, excavate land and create waste, roads and processing footprints.|Impervious surfaces change runoff and heat exchange; excavation and waste can alter topography and water quality. Rehabilitation can improve cover yet may not restore the original ecosystem structure.|Classroom image pair: a mine footprint grows from 2 to 6 km²; roads expand nearby; rehabilitation revegetates 1 km² using young trees.|Net disturbed area and revegetated area should be reported separately. Young plantings cannot immediately replace mature habitat or soil functions. Include indirect infrastructure effects beyond the mine boundary.|Revegetation instantly restores all pre-mining ecosystem functions.|cover-change|impervious;footprint;rehabilitation;indirect
Population, affluence and technology|Population growth increases demand, while affluence changes consumption per person. Technology can improve efficiency or enable greater extraction and consumption; effects depend on institutions and behaviour.|The same population can use different amounts of land through diets, housing density, waste and productivity. Land cover and the water cycle|Vegetation intercepts rain, returns water through evapotranspiration and can support infiltration. Clearing and hard surfaces can increase rapid runoff, depending on soil, slope, rainfall and drainage.|Reduced plant cover may lower interception and alter groundwater recharge; exposed soil can erode during intense rainfall. Urban drains move water quickly but can transfer downstream flood risk.|Classroom catchment model: 100 mm rain; forest intercepts 15 mm, infiltrates 55 and runs off 30. Urban cover intercepts 5, infiltrates 20 and runs off 75.|Runoff rises by 45 mm, equivalent to 450 m³ per hectare in this event model. Values are teaching assumptions, not universal coefficients. Storage, timing and evapotranspiration outside the event are simplified.|Clearing has exactly the same hydrological effect on every soil and slope.|water-cycle|interception;infiltration;runoff;evapotranspiration
Soil degradation and aquatic impacts|Soil erosion removes material; degradation also includes compaction, nutrient loss and salinity. Downstream sediment and nutrients can affect rivers, wetlands and coastal environments.|Bare soil and steep slopes increase some erosion risks; compaction reduces infiltration. Nutrient runoff may stimulate algal growth and oxygen depletion, while sediment can reduce light and smother habitats.|Classroom catchment: clearing exposes a slope above a reservoir; fertiliser is applied before heavy rain; turbidity and nutrient readings rise downstream.|Link vegetation removal to runoff and sediment transport, then fertiliser mobilisation to nutrient loading. A single event shows a possible mechanism; compare upstream controls and repeated measurements before assigning causation.|Soil degradation only matters at the cleared site.|water-cycle|erosion;compaction;sediment;eutrophication
Habitat, ecosystem services and fragmentation|Habitat supports species’ resources and conditions; fragmentation divides connected habitat into smaller patches. Ecosystem services are benefits such as pollination, water regulation and cultural values.|Roads and fields can isolate populations and increase edge effects. Corridors may improve movement for some species but can also spread fire or invasive organisms; design matters.|Classroom landscape: forest remains at 60% cover but is split into ten patches by roads, compared with an earlier single block.|Equal area does not imply equal habitat quality. Investigate patch size, connectivity, edge exposure and species needs. Assess services and cultural relationships alongside area, not as an automatic money value.|If total forest area is unchanged, biodiversity cannot decline.|ecosystem|fragmentation;connectivity;edge effect;ecosystem services
Urban heat islands and land cover|Urban heat islands occur when urban areas are warmer than surrounding areas under comparable conditions. Surface materials, reduced vegetation, geometry and waste heat influence storage and release of heat.|Dark hard surfaces absorb energy; reduced evapotranspiration removes a cooling pathway; buildings can restrict airflow. Surface temperature measured by satellite differs from air temperature at human height.|Classroom survey: shaded park air is 29°C and nearby paved-street air is 34°C at the same time and sensor height; a satellite records pavement surface at 47°C.|The observed air difference is 5°C, not 18°C. Control time, wind, exposure and instruments before generalising. Shade and vegetation can help but need water, maintenance and fair access.|Satellite surface temperature is identical to measured air temperature.|heat|heat island;surface temperature;air temperature;evapotranspiration
Climate patterns: latitude, circulation and rainfall|Temperature patterns reflect incoming solar energy, elevation, oceans and circulation. Rainfall patterns reflect moisture supply, uplift, atmospheric pressure and seasonal movement of circulation belts.|Warm rising air near the equator supports rain; subtropical descending air can favour dry conditions; mountains create wet windward slopes and drier rain shadows. These are broad patterns with regional exceptions.|Classroom climate transect: tropical wet lowland, subtropical dry interior and elevated cool plateau. Compare rainfall totals and monthly temperatures supplied by the teacher.|Use latitude to explain broad energy differences, then circulation and relief to explain exceptions. A climate graph needs long-term monthly data; a single storm or hot day is weather.|Latitude alone determines every place’s climate.|circulation|latitude;circulation;rain shadow;climate
Earth’s heat budget and the greenhouse effect|The heat budget balances incoming solar radiation with reflected shortwave and outgoing longwave radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some outgoing longwave energy, warming the surface and lower atmosphere.|The natural greenhouse effect makes Earth warmer than it would otherwise be. Additional greenhouse gases alter the energy balance until temperature and outgoing energy respond. Energy is not trapped forever.|Classroom model: 100 incoming energy units; 30 are reflected and 70 absorbed. At equilibrium the Earth system emits 70 units to space.|Increasing reflectivity reduces absorbed energy if other factors stay fixed. Increasing greenhouse gases affects outgoing longwave transfer. Keep system boundaries clear: top-of-atmosphere balance differs from surface exchange.|The greenhouse effect is caused by a hole in the ozone layer.|heat-budget|shortwave;longwave;greenhouse;energy balance
Carbon stores, fluxes and sequestration|Carbon stores include vegetation, soils, oceans, atmosphere and fossil deposits. Fluxes move carbon between stores; sequestration removes and stores carbon, while permanence describes how long it remains.|Photosynthesis transfers atmospheric carbon into biomass; respiration and decomposition return it. Burning fossil fuels introduces long-stored carbon to the active cycle; clearing can release carbon and reduce future uptake.|Classroom stand: growth removes 12 carbon units annually; respiration and decomposition release 8; fire releases an additional 10 this year.|Without fire, net uptake is 4 units; with the additional fire release, the stand is a net source of 6. A large store can still be a net source in a particular year. Specify the period and included fluxes.|Any forest is always a net carbon sink in every year.|carbon|store;flux;sequestration;net sink
Atmospheric circulation and the hydrological cycle|Differential heating drives large-scale atmospheric circulation, redistributing heat and moisture. The water cycle links evaporation, condensation, precipitation, storage and runoff.|Rising air cools and can condense moisture; descending air warms and can inhibit clouds. Seasonal shifts, oceans and terrain complicate idealised circulation cells. Land-cover change can modify moisture transfer.|Classroom mechanism: extensive vegetation loss reduces evapotranspiration in a moist region while bare surfaces alter energy exchange.|Explain reduced moisture return and a possible effect on downwind rain, but avoid claiming one parcel controls global rainfall. Connect local changes to regional circulation and seek observations or modelling evidence.|All rain falling in a region comes directly from the nearest ocean.|circulation|circulation;condensation;moisture recycling;scale
Natural and anthropogenic climate drivers|Natural drivers include volcanic aerosol changes and variations in solar input; orbital changes matter over much longer timescales. Human drivers include fossil-fuel emissions and land-use changes.|Large volcanic eruptions can produce short-term cooling through reflective aerosols. Increased greenhouse gases alter longwave balance. Timescale, direction and magnitude help test explanations.|Classroom comparison: a short cooling pulse follows a volcanic eruption, while a multi-decade warming trend accompanies rising greenhouse-gas concentrations.|Distinguish temporary forcing from persistent accumulation. IPCC concludes that human activities have caused recent global warming. Two natural processes and two human processes should be explained by mechanism, not listed as interchangeable causes.|A natural climate variation proves recent warming cannot be human-caused.|heat-budget|forcing;volcanic aerosol;solar;anthropogenic
Climate evidence over geological and recent time|Proxy records infer past climate indirectly; instruments measure selected variables directly. Geological-scale and recent evidence differ in resolution, geographic coverage and uncertainty.|Ice-core gases reveal past atmospheric composition; isotopic records provide temperature-related information. Recent instrumental records combine station and ocean measurements using adjustments and stated baselines.|Classroom evidence: a long proxy record shows broad variations; a recent thermometer series shows annual anomalies. Explain why they should not be compared as identical raw measurements.|State what each indicator measures, how it relates to climate and its limits. Multiple independent evidence types strengthen interpretation. An anomaly needs a defined reference period and is not the actual temperature of every place.|A temperature anomaly is the same as a place’s absolute temperature.|climate-chart|proxy;instrumental;baseline;anomaly
Rates of warming and scenario interpretation|Rate is change divided by elapsed time; a projection is conditional on assumptions, while a prediction implies an expected outcome. Scenarios explore plausible pathways rather than guarantee one future.|A global mean trend does not imply identical warming at every location. Compare baseline periods, datasets and uncertainty intervals before calculating or interpreting a rate.|Classroom series: mean anomaly rises from 0.40°C to 0.90°C over 25 years. Two projections differ because their emissions assumptions differ.|Change is 0.50°C; average rate is 0.02°C per year or 0.20°C per decade. This linear calculation does not establish future acceleration or a local forecast. Describe the scenario conditions explicitly.|A scenario is a guaranteed forecast regardless of human decisions.|climate-chart|rate;projection;scenario;uncertainty
Albedo and carbon feedbacks|Albedo is the fraction of incoming shortwave radiation reflected. Land-cover changes can alter albedo, carbon stores, evapotranspiration and roughness simultaneously, with competing effects.|Ice loss exposes darker surfaces and increases absorption, a positive feedback. Clearing can increase reflectivity but release carbon and reduce moisture transfer; the net effect depends on place and timescale.|Classroom calculation: incoming solar energy is 200 units; surface A reflects 20% and B reflects 40%. Climate impacts in natural environments|Climate changes affect species ranges, water conditions, fire regimes, glaciers and ocean ecosystems. Exposure, sensitivity and capacity to adapt determine impacts rather than temperature alone.|Warming can shift suitable conditions faster than some species can migrate. Heat and acidification are different stress pathways in oceans. Climate impacts in anthropogenic environments|Agriculture and urban settlements are exposed to changing heat, water availability and extremes. Effects depend on crops, infrastructure, livelihoods and adaptive capacity.|Heat can reduce labour productivity and affect crops; drought can increase irrigation demand; coastal flooding can damage assets. Adaptation may move risks between places or groups.|Classroom agricultural scenario: rainfall reliability falls, irrigation costs rise and a crop becomes less suitable. Three adaptation pathways|Adaptation reduces harm from actual or expected climate impacts. Water-demand management, heat-sensitive urban design and agricultural adjustment target different exposure or sensitivity pathways.|Efficient irrigation can reduce water used per unit output; shade and ventilation can reduce heat exposure; changed planting or crop choice can respond to seasons. Each can have limits, rebound effects or unequal access.|Classroom choices: retrofit water systems, add street shade and diversify crops. Budget is limited and drought conditions may intensify.|Discuss all three mechanisms, costs and beneficiaries. Prioritise using risk and equity, then monitor water demand, heat exposure and livelihood stability. Adaptation does not replace the need to limit future warming.|Adaptation reduces greenhouse-gas emissions by definition.|water-cycle|adaptation;heat design;diversification;limits
Two climate mitigation pathways|Climate mitigation reduces greenhouse-gas emissions or increases durable removals. Energy decarbonisation and protection of carbon-rich ecosystems target different sources and sinks.|Low-emission electricity can displace fossil generation; reduced clearing avoids emissions and preserves stores. Additionality, leakage and permanence matter when evaluating claimed carbon benefits.|Classroom plan: reduce fossil electricity use and protect forest; clearing shifts to an unprotected district while drought raises fire risk.|Electricity and land measures need system-wide monitoring. Count displacement, additionality and possible leakage; include fire risk and local livelihoods. Do not equate planting seedlings with immediate permanent offset of fossil emissions.|A planted tree immediately cancels any quantity of fossil emissions.|carbon|mitigation;additionality;leakage;permanence
Land-cover strategy one: protection and enforcement|Protecting remaining ecosystems can avoid conversion and retain carbon, habitat and water functions.
