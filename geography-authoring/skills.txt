Map conventions, atlas choices and sketch maps|A usable map states title, orientation, legend, scale and source, usually within a border. An atlas can supply location and thematic context, but its scale and date determine usefulness.|A sketch map simplifies features to support an argument; symbols and annotations should explain relationships rather than reproduce decoration. Identify two omissions that could distort interpretation.|Add a north arrow, legend and clear title; show relative location and meaningful annotations. Eastings, northings and grid references|Grid references identify positions on a mapped grid. Read eastings first, then northings. A four-figure area reference identifies a square; a six-figure reference estimates a point to tenths within it.|For a six-figure reference, interpolate within the square rather than reading the upper-right grid line. Accuracy depends on grid scale and symbol position. Preserve leading zeroes when present.|Synthetic map: a school lies 0.4 across from easting 23 toward 24, and 0.7 up from northing 61 toward 62.|Area reference is 2361; six-figure reference is 234617. Latitude, longitude and degrees–minutes|Latitude measures angular distance north or south of the equator; longitude measures angular distance east or west of the prime meridian. One degree contains 60 minutes, not 100.|A coordinate needs hemisphere or signed convention. Angular differences are not automatically equal ground distances: longitude distance changes with latitude. Latitude-longitude is different from a local grid reference.|Classroom point: 31°57′S, 115°52′E. Convert to decimal degrees and identify its hemispheres; these approximate coordinates locate the Perth area.|Latitude is −(31 + 57/60) = −31.95°; longitude is 115 + 52/60 ≈ 115.8667°. South is negative and east positive under this convention. Do not treat 31°57′ as 31.57°.|Minutes can be converted by dividing by 100.|australia-map|latitude;longitude;minutes;hemisphere
Compass directions and bearings|Compass direction expresses orientation; a bearing is an angle measured clockwise from north, normally written with three digits. Sixteen-point directions divide the compass into 22.5° sectors.|East is 090°, south 180° and west 270°. A reverse bearing differs by 180° with adjustment to the 000–359° range. Distinguish grid, true and magnetic north when a map supplies them.|Classroom route: from A to B the bearing is 067.5°, approximately ENE. Determine the return bearing and explain a possible limitation of a magnetic compass.|Return bearing is 247.5°, approximately WSW. In whole-degree notation accept about 248°. Magnetic and grid north may differ; apply the supplied declination if required. Do not invent a correction absent from the map.|Bearings are measured anticlockwise from east.|compass|bearing;ENE;reverse bearing;declination
Scale conversion, distance and travel time|Ratio scale 1:n means one map unit represents n of the same units on the ground. Convert units after multiplying; digital resizing can invalidate a stated ratio while a graphical scale resizes with the map.|At 1:50,000, 1 cm represents 500 m. A curved route should be measured along its path; straight-line distance is not necessarily travel distance. Time equals distance divided by speed under a constant-speed model.|Classroom task: a 6 cm route is measured on a map at its original 1:50,000 size. Walking speed is assumed to be 4 km/h.|Ground distance = 6 × 50,000 cm = 300,000 cm = 3 km. Time = 3/4 hour = 45 minutes. Stops, slope and surface may increase actual time. Use the supplied linear scale if the print size changes.|Six centimetres at 1:50,000 equals 30 km.|topographic|ratio scale;linear scale;distance;travel time
Area from maps and unit conversion|Area depends on the square of a length scale. One square kilometre is 1,000,000 m² or 100 hectares; one hectare is 10,000 m². Counting grid cells gives an estimate for irregular shapes.|At 1:50,000, 1 cm is 0.5 km, so 1 cm² represents 0.25 km². Edge cells can be estimated by halves; state uncertainty rather than implying perfect precision.|Classroom parcel measures 4 cm by 3 cm on a correctly sized 1:50,000 map. Find area in km² and hectares.|Ground dimensions are 2 km by 1.5 km, giving 3 km² or 300 ha. Multiplying map area by 50,000 without squaring the conversion is wrong. For a non-rectangular parcel, use a cell-count or polygon method.|An area scale is identical to the length scale.|topographic|area;hectare;conversion;estimate
Contours, landforms and slope shape|Contours join points of equal elevation. Close spacing indicates a steeper slope for the same contour interval; valleys and spurs have different contour shapes. Spot heights give point elevations.|A valley’s contour V usually points uphill; a spur’s projection points downhill. Concave slopes steepen uphill and flatten toward the foot; convex slopes are gentler near the top and steeper lower down.|Classroom profile from hilltop to foot: elevations 200, 190, 170, 140, 100 m at equally spaced horizontal positions.|Drops increase downhill: 10, 20, 30 and 40 m, showing a convex profile under the simplified sampling. Use contour spacing and flow direction together; closed contours may represent a hill or depression depending on symbols.|Every closed contour must be a hill, regardless of map symbols.|profile|contour;valley;spur;convex
Gradient ratios and cross sections|Average gradient compares vertical change with horizontal distance in the same units. Express 1:n by dividing horizontal distance by vertical change. A cross section plots elevations along a line through terrain.|The smaller the n in 1:n, the steeper the slope. Cross sections show relief and annotated cultural features; vertical exaggeration can make slopes appear steeper than reality.|Classroom route rises from 80 m to 200 m over 2.4 km horizontally. The cross-section horizontal scale is 1:50,000 and vertical scale 1:5,000.|Rise = 120 m; horizontal = 2,400 m; gradient = 1:(2,400/120) = 1:20. Vertical exaggeration = 50,000/5,000 = 10. Label both scales and do not infer actual slope angle from the exaggerated drawing alone.|A gradient of 1:100 is steeper than 1:20.|profile|gradient;cross section;vertical exaggeration;relief
Statistical maps: choropleth, dots and circles|A choropleth shades defined areas by a statistic; dot maps represent quantities or distributions; proportional circles vary symbol area to show magnitude. Isolines join equal values on a continuous surface.|Rates are often suitable for choropleths when areas have different populations; absolute counts can be misleading. Circle area, not radius, should be proportional to the value, so radius is proportional to the square root.|Classroom districts A and B each report 100 cases; A has 10,000 people and B has 50,000. A circle map must compare 100 with 400 units.|Rates are 10 versus 2 cases per 1,000 people. For values 100 and 400, radius ratio is 1:2, not 1:4. Describe classification and scale; do not infer individuals’ risks from area averages.|A larger absolute case count always means a higher per-person risk.|choropleth|choropleth;dot distribution;proportional circle;isoline
Photographs, radar, satellite images and change|Ground photographs show local detail but limited area; vertical aerial images support plan view; oblique images show perspective with variable scale. Satellite and radar products observe wider patterns with sensor-specific properties.|Image colour is not necessarily natural colour; radar responds to surface properties and geometry. Topographic maps add symbols, heights and names. Pairing images and maps can strengthen interpretation.|Classroom pair: two land-cover images taken in different seasons show a green field in one and brown in the other. A topographic map still classifies it as agricultural land.|A colour difference might reflect seasonal cropping, moisture or sensor processing rather than permanent clearing. Align location, date, season, scale and classification, and check field or official records. Direction must come from metadata or known features, not an assumed top edge.|Every brown pixel in a satellite image indicates deforestation.|cover-change|remote sensing;resolution;season;ground truth
Change statistics, descriptive statistics and frequency|Mean is sum divided by count; median is the middle ordered value; mode is the most frequent; range is maximum minus minimum. Percent change uses the initial value as the denominator.|Outliers can pull the mean away from typical observations; frequencies describe how often values or classes occur. A change in percentage points differs from relative percentage change.|Classroom journey minutes: 10, 12, 12, 16, 30. A service access rate rises from 40% to 60%.|Mean = 80/5 = 16; median 12; mode 12; range 20. Access rises 20 percentage points, a 50% relative increase. Report the statistic appropriate to the question and do not delete an outlier without justification.|A rise from 40% to 60% is always described accurately as a 20% increase.|data-chart|mean;median;mode;percentage points
Graphs, correlation and causal claims|Graph choice depends on data: lines for ordered time, bars for categories, histograms for grouped continuous values, scatterplots for association, and population pyramids for age-sex structure.|Correlation describes co-variation, not proof of cause. Shared drivers, reverse causation and sampling can produce associations. Climate graphs combine rainfall columns and temperature lines with separate axes.|Classroom scatterplot relates tree cover to temperature across six sites. Shadier sites are cooler but also differ in building density and measurement time.|A negative association supports further inquiry, not proof that trees alone caused the difference. Control time and instruments, include density and wind, and compare repeated sites. Do not join unrelated categories with a trend line.|A strong correlation establishes a causal mechanism without other evidence.|data-chart|scatterplot;histogram;correlation;confounder
Population pyramids, climate graphs and synoptic charts|A population pyramid shows age structure split by sex or gender categories defined by its source; a climate graph shows monthly temperature and rainfall; a synoptic chart shows pressure and weather systems at a time.|Compare units and denominators in pyramids; annual rainfall is the sum of monthly rainfall and temperature range is maximum minus minimum monthly mean. Close isobars suggest a stronger pressure gradient, not an exact wind speed.|Classroom climate data: monthly rain sums to 720 mm; warmest monthly mean is 26°C and coldest 14°C. A synoptic chart has closely spaced isobars near a low.|Annual rainfall is 720 mm and monthly temperature range 12°C. Explain seasonal pattern with actual monthly evidence. Pressure spacing supports stronger potential winds but local surface conditions and the chart’s time matter.|A single synoptic chart represents the long-term climate.|climate-chart|pyramid;climate graph;isobar;pressure gradient
GIS overlays, source quality and ethical fieldwork|GIS overlays combine locations and attributes, but coordinate systems, dates, resolution and definitions must align. Fieldwork provides local evidence and should use consistent methods with consent and privacy.|Reliability concerns consistency; validity concerns whether evidence answers the inquiry. Triangulation combines independent sources, while reference logs identify author, date, method and limitations.|Classroom overlay: old housing data and recent flood modelling use different boundaries. A survey of only volunteers with cars excludes some exposed residents.|Harmonise geography and date, validate the model and improve sampling access. Record methods and uncertainty; anonymise responses and avoid unsafe field sites. More precise digital output cannot repair invalid or biased inputs.|Combining many layers makes poor-quality data automatically valid.|gis|overlay;coordinate system;reliability;validity
