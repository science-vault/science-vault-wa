Hazards, risk and disasters|A hazard is a potentially damaging process; risk depends on the chance of an event and its consequences for exposed people and assets. A disaster is a severe disruption when impacts exceed a community’s capacity to cope.|Exposure locates people or assets in harm’s way; vulnerability describes susceptibility; capacity includes resources to prepare and recover. The same physical event can produce different losses in two places.|Classroom scenario: two coastal districts face the same cyclone. A has 8,000 residents, weak roofs and one road; B has 8,000 residents, stronger roofs and several evacuation routes.|A has greater vulnerability and less evacuation capacity, although population exposure is equal. Stronger buildings reduce damage rather than stopping cyclones. More routes can improve evacuation if warning, transport and shelter access also work.|A severe hazard always creates the same disaster everywhere.|risk|hazard;exposure;vulnerability;capacity
Atmospheric, hydrological and geomorphic hazards|Atmospheric hazards arise from weather processes; hydrological hazards involve water movement or availability; geomorphic hazards involve Earth materials and landforms. Boundaries can overlap in a compound event.|A tropical cyclone is atmospheric, river flooding is hydrological and a landslide is geomorphic. Drought develops through prolonged water shortage; bushfire involves fuel, weather and ignition rather than a single category alone.|Classroom scenario: intense cyclone rain saturates a hillside; soil fails into a river; downstream flooding affects houses.|Explain the sequence from atmospheric trigger to slope failure to hydrological consequence. Classification identifies the dominant process but should not hide interactions. Earthquakes and volcanic eruptions have tectonic causes, unlike a rainfall-triggered landslide.|Each event belongs to one isolated category with no linked hazards.|cascade|atmospheric;hydrological;geomorphic;compound
Ecological hazards and transmission pathways|Ecological hazards include biological threats such as infectious disease, invasive animals and water-borne illness, and chemical contamination. Identify the agent, pathway and exposed population.|Infection can spread through contact, vectors or contaminated water. An invasive species can disrupt food webs; chemicals may move through runoff and bioaccumulate. A hazard becomes a risk where a pathway links an agent to people or ecosystems.|Classroom scenario: floodwater reaches a damaged sewage system, while uncovered water containers support mosquitoes near homes.|Unsafe water can transmit some pathogens through ingestion; mosquitoes transmit dengue through bites. These require different controls: safe water and sanitation versus reducing mosquito breeding and exposure. Do not assume dengue is contracted by drinking floodwater.|Every disease after a flood is transmitted through drinking water.|dengue|ecological;pathway;vector;contamination
Magnitude, duration and spatial extent|Magnitude measures event intensity or size; duration is how long it lasts; spatial extent is the area affected. Frequency describes occurrence over time, while probability expresses likelihood within a stated period.|A high category cyclone may have a small intense core but widespread rain. A long drought may have lower daily intensity yet large cumulative losses. Never compare events using a category number alone.|Classroom data: Storm A affects 900 km² for 6 hours; drought B affects 24,000 km² for 18 months. Both interrupt food production.|The drought covers about 26.7 times the area and lasts far longer, but these dimensions do not alone establish which caused greater loss. Crop timing, water storage, population and livelihoods determine consequences.|The event with the greatest intensity must have the greatest total impact.|hazard-chart|magnitude;duration;extent;frequency
Recurrence intervals and probability|A recurrence interval is a long-run average interval between events at or above a specified magnitude; it is not a timetable. A 1-in-100-year event has a 1% annual exceedance probability under the assumed stationary model.|For independent years with constant annual probability p, the chance of at least one event in n years is 1 − (1 − p)^n. Climate or land-use changes can invalidate a stationary estimate.|Classroom calculation: a flood has a 2% annual exceedance probability. A household plans to remain for 30 years.|Annual probability 0.02 implies a 50-year average recurrence interval. Thirty-year probability is 1 − 0.98^30, approximately 45.5%. Two large floods can occur in consecutive years; independence and constant risk are modelling assumptions.|A 50-year flood cannot happen again until 50 years have passed.|hazard-chart|recurrence;probability;stationarity
Where hazards occur and why|A spatial pattern describes distribution; a temporal pattern describes change or timing. Explanations connect those patterns to physical and human processes rather than repeating where events occur.|Earthquakes often cluster near plate boundaries; tropical cyclones need warm oceans and suitable atmospheric conditions; disease patterns reflect climate, vectors, mobility and surveillance. Exposure patterns may differ from hazard patterns.|Classroom map: cyclone tracks cluster over tropical oceans, while a coastal city has expanding settlement on low-lying land.|Warm sea water and sufficient rotation help explain cyclone formation away from the equator. Settlement growth explains increased exposure but does not establish increased cyclone frequency. Report clustering, direction and an exception before explaining causes.|A map of losses is the same as a map of physical hazard frequency.|cyclone-map|spatial;temporal;distribution;clustering
Mitigation and preparedness|Mitigation reduces potential impacts; preparedness builds the ability to respond and recover before an event. Some actions contribute to both, so explain the mechanism rather than relying on a label.|Stronger roof connections reduce structural damage; evacuation drills, supplies and communication plans improve readiness. A warning only helps when people receive, understand and can act on it.|Classroom scenario: a coastal council can retrofit roofs or fund shelters, drills and transport for residents without cars.|Roof retrofit addresses structural vulnerability but does not remove flood exposure. Shelter access and drills reduce evacuation barriers but need reliable staffing and inclusive communication. Combining measures can cover different pathways to harm.|Issuing a warning guarantees every resident can evacuate.|risk|mitigation;preparedness;warning;capacity
Spatial technologies for hazard management|GIS combines geographically referenced layers; remote sensing observes conditions from a distance; GPS records locations. Each tool answers a different part of an inquiry.|Overlay elevation, housing, roads and predicted flood extent to identify exposed districts. Satellite images can track cloud systems or inundation; ground observations validate interpretation and reveal missing people or assets.|Classroom scenario: a flood overlay marks District A as high risk, but the housing layer is five years old and omits a new settlement.|The overlay is a planning aid, not certainty. Update the exposure layer, check flood-model assumptions and compare predicted water levels with field evidence. Missing settlement data can underestimate both population and evacuation demand.|A digital map is automatically accurate because a computer produced it.|gis|GIS;remote sensing;GPS;validation
Cyclone formation and structure|Tropical cyclones develop from organised thunderstorms over warm oceans when moisture, rotation, instability and relatively low vertical wind shear support a rotating low-pressure system.|Evaporation supplies moisture; condensation releases latent heat; rising air lowers surface pressure and draws in more air. The Coriolis effect supports rotation away from the equator; strong shear can disrupt organisation.|Worked model: warm moist air rises around a low-pressure centre; intense rain and wind concentrate in the eyewall around the eye.|The eye can have lighter winds, but dangerous eyewall conditions return as the system passes. Wind, rainfall flooding and storm surge are distinct impact mechanisms. Warm oceans are necessary for many systems but do not guarantee development.|The eye passing means the entire cyclone has ended.|cyclone|latent heat;eyewall;wind shear;storm surge

Yasi: physical processes and compound impacts|A cyclone’s pressure and winds can raise coastal water levels; waves add energy at the shoreline. Heavy rain can flood rivers and slopes independently of the wind maximum.|Low coastal elevation, coastal shape and tide timing influence inundation. A hillside or river catchment can experience impacts away from landfall. Attribute each consequence to its physical pathway.|Classroom Yasi-context scenario: an exposed coast faces surge, an inland catchment receives prolonged rain and crops face strong winds. These scenario values are not reported event measurements.|Explain surge as coastal inundation, rain as runoff and river flooding, and wind as mechanical damage. Avoid treating all damaged properties as evidence of one process. Yasi: environmental, economic and social impacts|Environmental impacts affect ecosystems; economic impacts affect production, assets and incomes; social impacts affect health, housing, access and wellbeing. Dengue: agent, vector and distribution|Dengue is a viral infection mainly transmitted by infected Aedes mosquitoes. The mosquito is the vector; the virus is the agent; people are hosts.|Temperature and standing water influence mosquito breeding, while movement can introduce the virus to new locations. Dense settlement does not produce the virus but can support contact between vectors and hosts.|Bangladesh depth study: WHO describes the 2023 outbreak and its spread. Use the dated outbreak report as event evidence, not as a statement about current cases.|Separate hazard presence from reported cases. Surveillance access and testing can change the observed distribution. Reducing mosquito breeding targets transmission; safe drinking water remains important but is not the dengue transmission pathway.|Dengue is caused by mosquitoes rather than a virus.|dengue|virus;Aedes;vector;host
Bangladesh 2023: scale and temporal evidence|An outbreak report is a dated snapshot. Counts, rates and denominators answer different questions, and the observation period must accompany any total.|WHO’s 11 August 2023 report describes 69,483 reported cases and 327 deaths from 1 January to 7 August. These are not full-year totals. A case-fatality ratio uses reported deaths divided by reported cases.|Calculate the approximate reported case-fatality ratio for this dated snapshot. Then explain why it is not the infection-fatality ratio.|327 ÷ 69,483 × 100 ≈ 0.47%. Undetected infections are absent from the case denominator; delayed reporting and unresolved cases affect interpretation. Describe event duration with the dates rather than assuming the epidemic stopped in August.|A case-fatality ratio measures deaths among every infected person.|hazard-chart|outbreak;denominator;case-fatality;reporting
Dengue vulnerability and human amplification|Water storage, waste, drainage, housing and access to health services can alter dengue exposure and outcomes. These interact with seasonal conditions and mosquito ecology.|Uncovered containers can create breeding sites; delayed diagnosis can increase the risk of severe outcomes. Dengue strategy one: source reduction|Source reduction removes or manages mosquito breeding sites. It can combine covered water containers, waste collection, drainage maintenance and community participation.|Reducing larvae lowers future adult mosquito abundance, but breeding sites can reappear after rainfall. Implementation needs coordination across households and public spaces, not a single clean-up.|Classroom trial: 80 of 200 inspected containers contain larvae initially; 30 of 200 do after a covered-container programme. Sites are inspected in similar seasonal conditions.|Positive-container share falls from 40% to 15%, a 25 percentage-point decline. This is not automatically a 25% relative decline or proof of fewer clinical cases. Repeat inspections and link results to surveillance while checking seasonal confounding.|One clean-up permanently removes all dengue risk.|dengue|source reduction;larvae;monitoring;confounding
