Globalisation and uneven interconnection|Globalisation is the growth of connections between places through flows of goods, services, capital, people and ideas. Connection intensity is uneven; distance still matters alongside networks and power.|Transport and telecommunications reduce some costs and delays, enabling activities to be coordinated across borders. They do not remove tariffs, language differences, infrastructure gaps or physical distance.|Classroom scenario: a Perth café purchases imported beans, uses overseas payment software and promotes itself online, while a remote producer has unreliable internet.|Trace material, financial and information flows separately. The café’s choices link it to distant places, but unequal connectivity can affect bargaining power and market access. Explain who benefits and who faces barriers.|Globalisation makes every place equally connected.|network|globalisation;flows;networks;unevenness
Transport and time–space compression|Time–space compression describes reductions in the time or cost of connecting places. It changes functional distance without changing their actual kilometres apart.|Containerisation standardises cargo handling; refrigeration preserves perishable goods; air transport speeds selected flows. Ports and logistics hubs concentrate movement and can create bottlenecks.|Classroom data: shipping takes 28 days and costs $0.40 per kg; air freight takes 3 days and costs $4 per kg. The product spoils after 10 days without cooling.|Shipping saves $3.60 per kg but requires an effective cold chain or a different product. Air transport improves speed at higher cost and typically higher emissions per tonne-kilometre. Choose using perishability, cost and environmental criteria.|Faster transport is always the most sustainable choice.|network|containerisation;cold chain;functional distance;logistics
Telecommunications and digital inequalities|Telecommunications allow information to move quickly and coordinate production, marketing and cultural exchange. Access includes infrastructure, affordability, skills and the freedom to participate.|Digital platforms can connect small enterprises to consumers and international organisations. Algorithms and platform ownership influence visibility, while unequal broadband access can limit participation.|Classroom scenario: two coffee cooperatives produce similar beans; one can publish traceable digital records and the other relies on a slow intermediary.|Digital records may improve access to buyers, but connectivity alone does not guarantee fair prices. Diffusion, adoption and adaptation|Diffusion is the spread of an idea or practice between places. Adoption accepts it; adaptation changes it to fit local needs. Expansion diffusion spreads outward while relocation diffusion moves with people.|Contagious diffusion spreads through contact; hierarchical diffusion can move through influential centres; migration carries practices to new destinations. Multiple processes can operate together.|Classroom football example: a sport arrives with migrants, spreads through major-city schools, then changes scheduling and uniforms to suit local climate and cultural expectations.|Identify relocation through migrants, hierarchy through schools and adaptation through changed practice. A sequence can show how a global element becomes locally distinctive. Do not equate adaptation with passive copying.|Cultural diffusion only produces identical practices everywhere.|diffusion|diffusion;adoption;adaptation;relocation
World cities as command and cultural nodes|World cities are important nodes in international networks of finance, corporate services, transport, communication and culture. Their influence is not determined solely by population size.|Corporate headquarters coordinate investment; specialised services support global business; media and institutions distribute ideas. Smaller influential cities can have stronger global functions than larger cities.|Classroom network: City A has 6 million people and major financial links; City B has 15 million but mainly regional services.|A can have a stronger command role despite a smaller population. Describe function, connectivity and hinterland relationships; avoid treating a world city as synonymous with a megacity. Both may face local inequality and environmental pressure.|Every megacity is automatically a leading world city.|network|world city;command function;node;connectivity
Social, economic and environmental globalisation impacts|Global integration can increase markets, employment and cultural contact while distributing costs unevenly. Sustainability considers social wellbeing, economic durability and environmental limits together.|An export boom may raise revenue yet concentrate profits among processors. Transport creates emissions; specialised production can pressure land or water. Cultural exchange can support creativity and threaten some local practices.|Classroom scenario: exports double, farm-gate prices barely rise, processing jobs increase and nearby forest is cleared for new production.|Evaluate each stakeholder and scale. Increased trade is evidence of integration but not proof of fair income or lower environmental impact. Link proposed standards to measurable livelihood and land-cover outcomes.|Rising exports prove that every stakeholder is better off.|impact|sustainability;stakeholder;trade-off;scale
Coffee as a global commodity|Coffee links tropical growing areas, traders, roasters, retailers and consumers. Production suitability and consumption demand create a geography of interdependence rather than a single local market.|Physical conditions shape cultivation; infrastructure, skills and markets shape processing and trade. FAO identifies climate change and the economic sustainability of production as important industry uncertainties.|Depth-study inquiry: use the linked FAO coffee resource to identify production and trade patterns. Build a source log recording year, unit, country coverage and whether data are beans, value or consumption.|A map of monetary exports differs from a map of tonnes produced. Coffee can be re-exported or processed outside its growing area. Keep measurement definitions consistent before comparing places or change over time.|A country exporting processed coffee must grow all of those beans.|coffee-map|commodity;production;consumption;re-export
Coffee supply chains and value capture|A supply chain links growing, processing, exporting, shipping, roasting and retailing. Value capture describes which actors retain revenue after their costs, not simply the final sale price.|Transformation, branding, distribution and market access can concentrate earnings downstream. Farmers’ income also depends on yield, quality, labour costs and bargaining arrangements.|Classroom prices per comparable kg: farm gate $4, export stage $7, roasted wholesale $15 and retail $28. These teaching prices do not include a complete cost account.|The retail-minus-farm-gate gap is $24, but it is not the retailer’s profit: costs, losses and processing intervene. Coffee production and consumption pattern change|Changing production and consumption patterns reflect climate suitability, investment, incomes, preferences and policies. Transport and technology in the coffee network|Container shipping, port systems, storage and telecommunications coordinate the movement of coffee and information. Improved links may change which places can participate in exports.|Tracking can connect batch quality and origin records to buyers; shipping reliability affects delivery and inventory. Poor last-mile roads can remain a barrier even when global ships are efficient.|Classroom proposal: a cooperative improves a farm-to-port road and adopts batch tracking; a competing proposal funds only online advertising.|The road targets physical access and loss risk; tracking targets information and trust. Governments and enterprises in coffee distribution|Governments shape trade through infrastructure, tariffs, standards and rural support. Enterprises choose sourcing, processing and market strategies. Their decisions affect production geography and access to demand.|A processing investment can create local jobs and retain more value, but needs skills, energy and buyers. Certification can change practices while imposing costs that some growers cannot afford.|Classroom case: a government funds rural roads; a roaster requires verified environmental standards; a cooperative seeks finance to meet them.|Explain how roads reduce access barriers and how buyer requirements change eligibility. Evaluate certification support against exclusion risks. Do not assume certification alone establishes higher net income or eliminates clearing.|Only consumers influence where a commodity is produced.|coffee|government;enterprise;standards;market access
Coffee adoption, adaptation and resistance|People and places can embrace imported products, adapt their use or resist their spread. Consumption practices reflect history, income, identity and local alternatives.|Local roasting styles and café cultures adapt a commodity; campaigns may resist unsustainable sourcing or high prices. Adoption is not an inevitable result of exposure to advertising.|Classroom scenario: a community café combines imported coffee with local food, while a campaign favours lower packaging waste and support for local tea producers.|Identify adaptation in the café and selective resistance in the campaign. Football as an element of culture|An element of culture includes shared practices, values, meanings and institutions. Football’s spread links migration, education, media, enterprise and local identity.|Rules can travel internationally while supporters, playing styles and participation arrangements develop local meanings. A global sport is a cultural practice as well as an industry.|Depth-study inquiry: trace football from established centres to a selected local community. Use FIFA development material as evidence of institutional activity and recognise its promotional perspective.|Describe what diffuses: rules, skills, symbols, competition formats and fandom. Distinguish geographic spread from growth in participation. Do not use commercial audience size alone to represent cultural acceptance.|A sport is only an economic commodity, not culture.|football-map|culture;practice;identity;participation
Football diffusion through migration and hierarchy|Migration can carry football to new places; institutions and major centres can spread it through hierarchical networks. Local contact can then sustain expansion diffusion.|A club founded by migrants may introduce practice; schools, leagues and national associations formalise access. Routes vary across places and periods, so one universal origin story is inadequate.|Classroom chronology: migrant workers begin games in a port, a school league forms in the capital and smaller towns establish teams later.|Relocation explains the port introduction; hierarchy explains the capital’s institutional role; local expansion explains neighbouring towns. Media, technology and football’s cultural reach|Broadcasting, streaming and social platforms can increase visibility, connect supporters and circulate symbols. Media influence includes whose competitions are shown and who can afford access.|Algorithms, rights agreements and language affect audience reach. Mobile highlights can connect supporters quickly, yet participation still requires facilities, time and safe access.|Classroom data: online views rise from 2 million to 5 million while registered local players rise from 20,000 to 22,000.|Views rise 150%; registered participation rises 10%. Institutions, corporations and football networks|Transnational sporting institutions coordinate rules, tournaments and development; corporations sponsor, broadcast and market events.
