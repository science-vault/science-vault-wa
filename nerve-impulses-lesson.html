PART 02 / 10

What is a nerve impulse?

A nerve impulse is an electrochemical change that travels along a neurone’s membrane.

Ion movement changes the voltage across the membrane. A brief change in membrane potential is an action potential. Successive action potentials carry the message along the axon.

Transmission speed

Myelination and a larger axon diameter allow faster transmission.

Key vocabulary

Membrane potential · depolarisation · repolarisation · hyperpolarisation · action potential · propagation

PART 03 / 10

Meet the membrane

Ion channels and the sodium–potassium pump have different jobs.

  • Leak channels allow ions to move down their electrochemical gradients.
  • Voltage-gated channels open or close in response to membrane voltage.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, maintaining the concentration gradients.

PART 04 / 10

Before the stimulus

At rest, the inside is negative relative to the outside: approximately −70 mV.

There is more Na⁺ outside and more K⁺ inside the neurone. Voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels are closed. Leak channels and the concentration gradients maintained by the pump help establish the resting membrane potential.

PART 05 / 10

Sodium moves in

A sufficient stimulus brings the membrane to threshold, approximately −55 mV.

  1. Local stimulation opens some Na⁺ channels and changes the membrane potential.
  2. If threshold is reached, many voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open.
  3. Na⁺ enters rapidly. The inside becomes positive relative to the outside.
All or nothing

If threshold is not reached, no action potential occurs. A stronger stimulus does not make each action potential larger; it can increase their frequency and recruit more neurones.

Think: why does a hammer hurt more than a slice of bread?

A stronger stimulus can produce more frequent action potentials and activate more sensory neurones. Each action potential in a given neurone remains an all-or-nothing event.

PART 06 / 10

Potassium moves out

The membrane potential falls towards its resting value.

  1. At the peak, Na⁺ channels become inactivated and voltage-gated K⁺ channels are open.
  2. K⁺ moves out of the neurone.
  3. The inside becomes negative again.

PART 07 / 10

Hyperpolarisation and return to rest

Potassium channels close slowly, so the membrane briefly becomes more negative than at rest.

This is hyperpolarisation. As voltage-gated K⁺ channels close, the membrane returns towards resting potential. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump continues to maintain and restore the ion concentration gradients.

Keep the roles clear

K⁺ leaving through channels causes repolarisation. The pump maintains the gradients; it is not the direct cause of the rapid falling phase.

PART 08 / 10

Why the wave travels forwards

A section of membrane needs time to recover after an action potential.

During the absolute refractory period, inactivated Na⁺ channels cannot immediately reopen. During the relative refractory period, a stronger stimulus is needed. The region just behind a travelling impulse is recovering, helping prevent immediate backward re-excitation.

Propagation

Local currents from an active region bring the neighbouring resting membrane to threshold. A new action potential is generated there, and the process repeats down the axon.

Like falling dominoes, one region triggers the next. The membrane behind the wave is refractory.

PART 09 / 10

From node to node

In a myelinated axon, action potentials are regenerated at the nodes of Ranvier.

Myelin insulates the membrane between nodes. Local current spreads quickly beneath the sheath, bringing the next node to threshold. The action potential appears to “jump” from node to node: saltatory conduction.

Why it matters

Saltatory conduction makes impulse transmission faster than continuous conduction along an unmyelinated axon.
